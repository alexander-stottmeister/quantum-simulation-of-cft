A · The three scales

A CFT has no mass gap and therefore no length scale of its own. The scale of the simulation problem is set instead by the reader’s choice: \(M\), how well localized the observables have to be, and \(N\), how fine the simulated lattice is. The error depends on neither alone but on the distance between them, the renormalization depth \(r=N-M\).

What each scale is for

\(N\), the ultraviolet cutoff
What the quantum computer actually holds: \(n=2^{N+1}\) sites on \(2n=2^{N+2}\) qubits. Everything in the resource count is a function of \(n\).
\(M\), the observation scale
Where the observables live. \(\mathfrak A_M\) is the algebra the correlator is taken in, and \(\alpha^M_N\) embeds it into the simulated lattice. The Letter’s Complexity paragraph takes \(M=N-1\), which fixes the largest observation scale the register can host — not the accuracy.
\(r=N-M\), the depth
The number of renormalization steps between the two. The exact correlator is the limit \(r\to\infty\) at fixed \(M\); \(\delta\) bounds how far the finite-\(r\) approximant is from it.

Why the error is a function of \(r\). The lattice and continuum vacua are both quasi-free, so the vacuum error is a polynomial in matrix elements of the one-particle symbols, and along the momentum-cutoff route the deviation is a sine of the ratio \(\varepsilon_N/\varepsilon_M=2^{-(N-M)}\) — first order on the full two-component algebra, second order on the chiral algebra, because there the chiral entanglement of the finite-scale vacuum is the obstruction.

The dynamics term is not a function of \(r\) alone: it is \(\tfrac16 d_B\Theta_M|t|\,4^{-N}\), and \(\Theta_M\) grows with the observation scale roughly like \(2^{3.5M}\). So climbing \(M\) at fixed depth costs accuracy through the dynamics term even while the vacuum term stays put — which is why the near-term figure says scale-5 observables would need \(N\approx10\) where scale-3 observables need \(N=6\).
